# Mushroom knowledge base v2 (visual identification focus)

Short educational notes for retrieval (RAG). Each section starting with "## " is one retrievable chunk. They are written in the vocabulary of photo descriptions (cap colour, underside, stipe, habitat) so that a description of an image retrieves the right notes. These are brief summaries, not a field guide: verify against a trusted field guide and cite your sources. Never add preparation or dosage instructions for toxic species.

## Reading the underside of a mushroom: gills, ridges and pores
True gills are thin, sharp-edged plates like the pages of a book, mostly unbranched. Gills can be crowded or widely spaced, thin or thick and waxy. Ridges (false gills) are blunt, rounded, vein-like folds that fork repeatedly and run down the stem, as in chanterelles. Pores are a spongy surface of tiny holes, as in boletes. Decurrent means the gills or ridges run down the stipe. If the underside is not visible in the photo, these groups cannot be separated and the confidence must be low.

## Photo views and what is missing
A top-down view shows only the cap, so the underside and stipe are missing. A view from the side shows the stipe but may hide the gills. A photo of a cut mushroom shows flesh colour and colour changes. Always state which of cap, hymenium (underside) and stipe are visible, and name the missing features that would be needed: the stipe base, a ring, the underside, the habitat, and the spore print. A single cap seen from above is not enough to identify most species.

## Parasol mushroom (Macrolepiota procera)
A large, tall mushroom of grassland, pastures, clearings, roadsides and forest edges. The cap starts egg-shaped, then opens flat like a parasol, often 10 to 25 cm or more across. It is pale cream to tan with brown scales arranged in rings and a smooth, darker brown central bump (umbo). The stipe is tall and slender with a snakeskin-like pattern of brown bands, a bulbous base, and a large ring that can slide along the stipe. The gills are white, crowded and do not touch the stipe. Seen from above only, a large flat scaly cap with a dark central umbo suggests a parasol but cannot confirm it, because the stipe, ring and gills are not visible.

## Parasol look-alikes: small Lepiota, shaggy parasol, Amanita
Small Lepiota species with scaly caps are usually under about 8 cm across, and some, such as Lepiota brunneoincarnata, contain amatoxins and are deadly. The shaggy parasol (Chlorophyllum rhacodes) has a shaggy cap, a smooth stipe without snakeskin pattern, and flesh that turns orange-red when cut; it upsets some people's stomachs. Chlorophyllum molybdites has green spores and is poisonous. Amanita species have a volva at the base. Size cannot be judged from a photo without a scale. Identification of a parasol-like mushroom requires the stipe pattern, the movable ring, the gills and the spore print.

## Chanterelle (Cantharellus cibarius)
Egg-yolk to golden yellow throughout: cap, ridges and stipe are similar in colour. The cap is funnel-shaped with a wavy, rolled margin. Under the cap are blunt, forked, vein-like ridges running far down the stipe, not true gills. The flesh is solid, whitish to pale yellow, tears in strips, and smells fruity like apricot. The stipe is solid and tapers toward the base. It grows scattered or in groups on the ground in forests, often on moss, with trees. It is a prized edible, but never state that it is safe from a photo, because look-alikes exist.

## False chanterelle (Hygrophoropsis aurantiaca)
Cap orange to orange-brown, often darker in the centre, soft and slightly velvety, with a rolled margin. The underside has true, thin, crowded gills that fork regularly and run down the stem, deeper orange than the cap. The flesh is thin and soft, the stipe may become hollow, and there is no apricot smell. It grows on conifer litter, humus and rotting wood. It is generally considered not worth eating and can upset the stomach. Compared with a chanterelle, look for thin crowded true gills, a darker orange colour and a velvety cap.

## Jack-o'-lantern (Omphalotus)
Orange mushrooms with true thin, sharp, unforked gills running down the stem. They typically grow in dense clusters, with stipes joined at the base, on wood or on buried roots and stumps, not as scattered single mushrooms on soil. The stipe base often tapers into the wood. It is poisonous and causes severe vomiting and cramps. Compared with a chanterelle: chanterelles have blunt forked ridges, a fruity smell, and grow on the ground; Jack-o'-lanterns have sharp gills and grow in clusters on wood.

## Orange and yellow mushrooms with gills running down the stem: how to tell them apart
Chanterelle: golden yellow all over, blunt forked ridges the same colour as the cap, apricot smell, on forest soil. False chanterelle: orange, thin crowded true gills that fork, velvety cap darker in the middle. Jack-o'-lantern: orange, sharp true gills, in dense clusters on wood. Pine spike (Chroogomphus): orange-brown, thick widely spaced waxy gills that turn smoky grey-brown, tapering stipe, under pine. The colour and spacing of the underside and the habitat (conifer needles, moss, wood) are the main clues.

## Chroogomphus (pine spikes, Chroogomphus rutilus)
Cap dull copper, orange-brown or ochre, convex with a small bump, smooth and slightly slimy or shiny when wet. The gills are thick, waxy, widely spaced and run down the stem; they start pale ochre and turn smoky grey-brown to blackish as the spores ripen. The stipe is thick, tapers toward the base, is ochre to orange with faint cobweb-like veil remnants, and the flesh at the base is yellow. It grows with pine, often among pine needles and moss. It can be mistaken for a bolete or a chanterelle at a glance, but it has gills, not pores. Generally regarded as edible but of modest quality; confirm with an expert.

## Slimy spike cap (Gomphidius) and slippery jack (Suillus)
Gomphidius glutinosus is related to Chroogomphus: slimy grey-brown cap, whitish flesh and grey decurrent gills, with a white stipe that has a yellow base. Chroogomphus has orange flesh and an orange-brown colour. Suillus (slippery jack) is a bolete that grows with conifers and has pores, often with a slimy cap and sometimes a ring. These all share conifer habitats, so the underside decides: gills for Chroogomphus and Gomphidius, pores for Suillus.

## Boletes (Boletus and relatives)
Boletes have a cap with a spongy layer of pores under it instead of gills, and a thick, firm stipe, sometimes with a net-like pattern near the top. Many are edible; some are bitter or poisonous. A bitter bolete (Tylopilus felleus) has pinkish pores and a very bitter taste. Boletes with red or orange pores and flesh that turns blue quickly need expert identification. If the underside of the cap is not visible, a bolete cannot be separated from a gilled mushroom with a similar brown cap and thick stipe.

## Webcaps (Cortinarius): the genus
Cortinarius is a huge genus of mushrooms with rusty-brown spores. Young specimens have a cobweb-like veil (cortina) between cap and stipe. The gills start pale, violet or ochre and turn rust-brown as the spores ripen. The stipe is often fibrous or streaked, sometimes with bands or a rusty zone from cortina remnants. Caps are frequently orange-brown, rust, or reddish brown and dry to slightly silky. They grow on the forest floor with trees. Species cannot be separated from a photo, and several are deadly poisonous, so no Cortinarius should be eaten.

## Deadly webcaps (Cortinarius rubellus and Cortinarius orellanus)
Cortinarius rubellus has a pointed, orange-brown, finely scaly cap, rusty gills and a yellowish-brown stipe with yellowish bands, in coniferous forest on acidic mossy ground. Cortinarius orellanus has an orange to orange-brown cap and rusty gills, typically in deciduous forest. Both contain orellanine, which damages the kidneys. Symptoms are delayed, usually 2 to 4 days and sometimes up to two weeks or more, so poisoning can be missed. Anyone who may have eaten one needs urgent medical attention.

## Brown and rust-coloured gilled mushrooms on the forest floor: look-alikes
Rusty-brown gills and a brown fibrous stipe point to a rust-spored group: Cortinarius (webcaps, many toxic or deadly), Galerina (small, on wood, deadly amatoxins), Gymnopilus (rusty-orange spores, bitter, on wood), Hebeloma (poison pies, whitish-clay, upsets the stomach) and Inocybe (small, fibrous, cracking caps, many contain muscarine). The spore print colour and the habitat (soil versus wood) help separate them. Without these, the safe conclusion is a genus-level guess with low confidence and a clear warning not to eat it.

## Fly agaric (Amanita muscaria)
A red cap, often with white warts, white gills, a ring on the stipe, and a bulbous base with scaly rings. It contains ibotenic acid and muscimol, which affect the nervous system. Poisoning can cause nausea, drowsiness, confusion, delirium, hallucinations and muscle twitching. It is rarely fatal, but poisoning can be serious and needs medical attention. It is not the same as the deadly death cap. Do not give preparation, cooking, drying or dosage instructions.

## Death cap (Amanita phalloides)
Cap greenish, olive or yellowish, with white gills, a ring on the stipe, and a sack-like volva at the base that may be underground. It causes most fatal mushroom poisonings. The toxins are amatoxins, which damage the liver. Symptoms can be delayed by 6 to 24 hours, then include severe vomiting and diarrhoea, and the person may seem to recover before organ failure sets in. Anyone who may have eaten it needs emergency care immediately (112). General rule: white gills plus a ring plus a volva-like base means do not eat.

## Inkcaps (Coprinus and Coprinopsis)
Inkcaps have bell-shaped caps and gills that turn black and dissolve into an inky liquid as they age. The common inkcap (Coprinopsis atramentaria) can cause an unpleasant reaction (flushing, palpitations, nausea) if alcohol is consumed shortly before or after eating it. Other inkcaps are similar, so identification matters.

## How to fill in the JSON fields honestly
visible: list only the parts clearly shown among cap, hymenium (the underside: gills, ridges or pores) and stipe. A top-down view is ["cap"] only. confidence: below 0.4 when the underside or stipe is missing, and lower when look-alikes include toxic species; do not give a high value for a genus-level guess. edible: true only for a species that is well known as edible and confidently identified; for any Cortinarius, Amanita, or unclear mushroom use false. When a mushroom could be a toxic look-alike, say so in the summary.

## General foraging safety
Never eat a wild mushroom based only on a photo or a chatbot. Collect the whole mushroom including the base, note habitat and nearby trees, and take several specimens at different ages. Have the finds checked by a qualified person, such as a local mycological society or a mushroom inspection service. Avoid eating any mushroom you are not sure of. Several poisonous species have delayed symptoms, so feeling fine after eating does not mean it was safe. In suspected poisoning, call emergency services (112 in Sweden and the EU) or poison information, and keep a sample of the mushroom if possible.
